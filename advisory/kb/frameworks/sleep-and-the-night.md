# Sleep and the night

## What question it answers

*Two questions the sport keeps merging: how much does sleep matter in training, and what is my plan
for the second night of the race?*

They are separate problems and only the first has good evidence.

## Part 1 — Sleep as a training input

Sleep is the highest-yield recovery intervention available and the one most often traded away for a
5 a.m. session. Partial sleep deprivation has been shown to impair recovery from hard training;
inadequate sleep is associated with higher injury rates, impaired glucose metabolism, blunted muscle
repair and degraded cognition.

The operative consequence for an amateur is uncomfortable: **an early alarm that costs an hour of
sleep to gain an hour of easy running is frequently a net loss.** That trade is made weekly by most
time-constrained athletes and is almost never evaluated. It belongs in the same budget as the
training hours themselves.

## Part 2 — The race night

Mountain 100-milers run roughly 20–24 hours for the fastest and up to about 48 for most of the
field, so most finishers cross at least one night and some cross two. Survey data on mountain
ultramarathon finishers finds that not all runners sleep during a race, and those who do rely on
**brief naps**. The figures are in [[sleep-in-mountain-ultramarathon-finishers]].

Two findings are worth carrying into a plan:

1. **Sleep extension before the race is associated with faster finishing.** Runners who extended
   sleep pre-race finished faster than those who did not. Association, in a survey — but it is a
   cheap intervention with no downside, which makes the evidentiary bar for acting on it low.
2. **Sleep deprivation shows up first in pacing and decision-making, not in legs.** An earlier shift
   in race pacing under sleep deprivation predicts later performance. What degrades is judgement,
   and judgement is what the second night is made of.

## Inputs

- Expected time on course, and where the night or nights fall relative to the start.
- The athlete's habitual sleep, honestly recorded for two weeks. Not their estimate.
- Whether the race allows a crewed stop, and where.

## Steps

1. **Bank sleep for the week before.** Extend, don't merely protect. It is free.
2. **Write a night plan before the race, in the form of triggers rather than intentions.** "If I am
   weaving, or I have hallucinated twice, or I have been awake 24 hours and I am below X pace, I
   take a 20-minute nap at the next crew point."
3. **Nap short and early rather than long and late.** Short naps are what finishers actually report
   using. A long stop cools the athlete, stiffens them, and is much harder to leave.
4. **Plan the caffeine deliberately** and know that it borrows rather than repays. Save it for the
   dark hours rather than spending it at hour four.
5. **Delegate the decision.** The crew or pacer holds the night plan, because the athlete at hour
   thirty is the least qualified person present to decide whether the athlete should nap.
6. **Never make an irreversible decision in the dark.** See [[the-dnf-decision]] — this is the
   single most useful rule in this corpus and it follows directly from the finding that judgement
   goes before legs.
7. **After the race, expect subjective wakefulness back within about two days and physiological
   recovery to take much longer.** Confusing the two is how the post-race injury happens.

## Output

A written night plan with triggers, a nap location, a caffeine schedule, and a named person holding
it. Plus a pre-race sleep-extension week in the calendar.

## How to tell it is being applied wrongly

- **The night plan is "push through".** That is not a plan, it is the absence of one, and it will be
  executed by someone whose judgement is impaired.
- **Sleep is traded for training without the trade being counted.** See Part 1.
- **Caffeine is used as the whole strategy.** It masks the signal that the nap decision depends on.
- **A survey of finishers is read as evidence about the field.** The people whose night went worst
  are missing from the sample — see [[sleep-in-mountain-ultramarathon-finishers]] and
  [[pacing-by-effort]] for how the same survivorship problem distorts pacing advice.
