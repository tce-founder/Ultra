# Vertical gain and its substitutes

## What question it answers

*The race has 6,000 m of climbing and I live somewhere flat, or it is January. What actually
substitutes, and for which part of the demand?*

## Split the demand before choosing a machine

The mistake this page exists to prevent is treating "vert" as one thing. A mountain ultra imposes at
least four separable demands, and the indoor options cover them very unevenly:

| Demand | What it is | Best indoor substitute | How good |
| ------ | ---------- | ---------------------- | -------- |
| **Climbing aerobic cost** | Sustained high oxygen cost in a hip- and calf-dominant pattern | Stair machine (StepMill), incline treadmill, climbing machine | Good — this is what the machines do |
| **Hiking economy under a steep grade** | Power-hiking posture, pole use, cadence at 20–40% grade | Stair machine, treadmill at max grade | Fair. Poles are usually impossible indoors |
| **Eccentric descending load** | Quadriceps braking, thousands of repetitions, joint impact | **Nothing.** No standard machine descends | None — this is the gap |
| **Terrain skill and footing** | Uneven ground, rock, roots, fatigue-degraded proprioception | Nothing | None |

**The asymmetry is the whole point of this page.** An indoor winter can maintain and even build the
climbing half of a mountain athlete and leaves the descending half completely untrained. Races are
lost on the descents — that is where the eccentric damage accumulates and where the late-race
walking starts. An athlete who arrives with a strong indoor-built climbing engine and untrained
quadriceps has a specific, predictable failure at roughly the point the course turns down.

## The modalities

**Incline treadmill.** Gradient typically caps at 12–15%, a few models at 25–40%. Exact vertical
arithmetic. Can also serve heat adaptation, night-simulation and fuelling rehearsal because the
environment is controllable. Its limit is the gradient ceiling: at 15% the movement is still running
or fast walking, not the deep hip flexion of steep hiking.

**Stair machine / StepMill.** Effective gradient far steeper than a treadmill can reach, and
continuous rather than intervalled. Higher oxygen cost and heart rate than incline walking at
comparable perceived effort. Closest available match to sustained steep hiking. Its limits: fixed
step height and cadence, a handrail that silently offloads a large fraction of bodyweight, and
vertical figures that are not comparable between machines.

**Climbing machine (VersaClimber and similar).** Adds upper-body work, so total energy expenditure
per minute is high. That makes it excellent conditioning and *less* specific: a mountain runner
climbing with poles does use the upper body, but not in this pattern, and the leg work per minute is
lower than the whole-body cost suggests.

## Steps

1. **Compute the race's vertical demand** and the athlete's current weekly vertical, both in metres,
   both with the derivation stated.
2. **Choose the modality by demand, not by availability.** Sustained steep climbing → stair machine.
   Controlled environment, exact vertical, or a second variable (heat, fuelling) → treadmill.
   General aerobic conditioning under an injury restriction → climbing machine.
3. **Never hold the handrail** on a stair machine except to steady, and record whether you did. It
   is the single largest source of overstated indoor vertical.
4. **Protect the descent separately and deliberately.** If descents are unavailable, the substitute
   is eccentric strength work — see [[strength-training-for-ultrarunners]] — plus the earliest
   possible reintroduction of real downhill running, built gradually. This is not optional and it is
   not covered by any machine.
5. **Convert conservatively.** Treat indoor vertical as worth less than outdoor vertical for
   planning purposes rather than more. The direction of the error matters: overstating indoor vert
   produces an athlete who believes they are prepared.

## Output

Weekly vertical in metres with the modality and derivation named, and a separate, explicit answer to
"where is the descending load coming from".

## How to tell it is being applied wrongly

- **Indoor and outdoor vertical are summed into one number.** They were measured differently and one
  of them was probably measured by a machine that assumed a step height.
- **The handrail is load-bearing.** Common, rarely recorded, and it can remove a large share of the
  work.
- **The descent is assumed to take care of itself.** It does not, and this is the failure mode this
  page is most concerned with — see [[pacing-by-effort]] for how it presents in a race.
- **A machine is credited with terrain skill.** Footing under fatigue is trained on ground.
