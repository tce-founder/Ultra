# Strength training for ultrarunners

## What question it answers

*Is time in a gym worth the hours it takes from running, and if so, what kind of gym time?*

## The finding

Systematic reviews with meta-analysis in middle- and long-distance runners find that strength
training improves running economy, with the effect concentrated in **high-load** and **combined**
(heavy plus plyometric) programmes. Submaximal running economy improvements of roughly **3–5%** are
reported. Submaximal-load and isometric-only programmes did not show a significant effect. Higher
frequencies (2–3 sessions per week) outperformed once-weekly. The figures and their caveats are
recorded in [[strength-training-running-economy-meta-analyses]].

**What this does not say.** It does not say strength training prevents injury — that is a separate
and much messier literature. It does not say it makes you faster over 100 miles. And it does not say
it works at ultra intensities on gradients, because nobody measured that.

## Why an ultrarunner might still want it, for reasons the trials do not cover

Three arguments, each stated at its real standing:

1. **Economy under fatigue (`established` mechanism, `asserted` application).** A more economical
   runner spends less of a fixed energy budget per kilometre. In a race where the budget is the
   binding constraint, that compounds over hours in a way it cannot over 10 km. This is an
   extrapolation and is offered as one.
2. **Eccentric tolerance for descending (`experienced`).** Mountain ultras destroy quadriceps
   eccentrically, and eccentric damage is what turns a good race into a walk at 70 km. Loading the
   tissue deliberately in a gym is more controllable than loading it accidentally on a mountain.
3. **The tissue that fails is not the tissue that gets tired (`experienced`).** Tendon, bone and
   connective tissue adapt on a slower clock than the cardiovascular system, which is why fitness
   arrives before durability and why the injury lands in the good block rather than the bad one.

## Inputs

- Two to three hours a week that are genuinely available — this comes out of the same budget as
  running, see [[periodization-for-ultras]].
- A movement screen or a coach, if load is going above bodyweight and the athlete has not lifted.
- An honest injury history.

## Steps

1. **Heavy, low-rep, few exercises.** The evidence favours high external load. Squat, deadlift or
   hinge, single-leg work, calf/soleus loading. Three to six sets, low repetitions, long rests.
2. **Twice a week, all year.** The frequency finding is one of the more robust parts of the result,
   and strength detrains fast. A block that stops when the running volume rises has bought nothing
   for the race.
3. **Add plyometrics only once heavy work is tolerated.** The combined format is where the economy
   effect is strongest, and it is also where the achilles injuries are.
4. **Separate it from hard running.** Same day as a hard session, or a full day away — not the day
   before the long run.
5. **Load the descent specifically** in the specific block: downhill repeats on a runnable grade,
   built gradually. See [[vertical-gain-and-its-substitutes]] for why indoor climbing machines do
   not cover this and cannot.

## Output

Two sessions a week, four to six movements, load recorded, progressed. Not a circuit.

## How to tell it is being applied wrongly

- **Light and long.** High-rep bodyweight circuits are the format the meta-analyses found did *not*
  improve economy. They are not harmless — they cost the hours and buy the fatigue.
- **It is dropped when running volume rises.** That is precisely when it is doing the most.
- **It is sold as injury prevention.** The economy evidence is good; the injury-prevention evidence
  is much weaker, and stating the second with the confidence of the first is the `authority`
  laundering this schema exists to stop.
- **Core work substitutes for leg strength.** They are different interventions with different
  evidence.
