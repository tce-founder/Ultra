# Descending

> **Why this page exists.** The corpus carried fifteen framework pages and the tag
> `downhill-eccentric` appeared on exactly one of them — a page about indoor climbing machines,
> where descending is mentioned only as the thing those machines cannot train. For a mountain-ultra
> corpus that is a hole where the primary failure mode should be.

## The mechanism, which is not in dispute

On a descent the quadriceps work **eccentrically**: they lengthen under load, acting as a brake
against gravity rather than a motor. Eccentric contraction produces far more mechanical damage per
unit of metabolic cost than concentric work does. This is why a descent can feel
cardiovascularly trivial and still end a race, and it is why heart rate and perceived effort —
adequate controls on a climb, per [[pacing-by-effort]] — do not govern descending at all.

The practical consequence is counterintuitive: **the terrain that costs you the least to run is
the terrain that does you the most damage.**

## What is actually measured

| Finding | Grade |
| ------- | ----- |
| A **single** bout of downhill running attenuated fatigue from a level run performed **fourteen days later** | VERIFIED — controlled study |
| Repeated downhill bouts lowered creatine kinase and myoglobin on the second bout, consistent with reduced muscle damage | VERIFIED |
| Ten sessions of eccentric-biased work **reduced quadriceps soreness** after a standardised downhill bout | VERIFIED |
| Five or ten sessions did **NOT** change central or peripheral **fatigue** responses | VERIFIED — and see below |

This is the **repeated-bout effect**, and the first row is the most useful thing on this page: a
single downhill exposure buys weeks of protection against the next one. **Frequency beats volume.**
Regular modest descent exposure is worth more than occasional large doses, and it is the argument
for descent work being a year-round thread rather than a block.

## The counterpoint that matters most, and it is uncomfortable

**Eccentric conditioning is measured to reduce soreness and damage markers. It is measured NOT to
reduce the fatigue response.** Those are different outcomes, and the one a racer cares about —
*could I still run?* — sits closer to fatigue than to soreness.

So conditioning will make descents hurt less the next morning. The evidence does **not** support a
promise that it will let you hold pace down a mountain at hour twenty. Anyone selling eccentric
training as the fix for losing time on descents is extrapolating past the data.

**What that leaves.** If conditioning does not fully explain pace loss on descents, the remainder is
**skill and pacing** — which is exactly where it is hardest to find evidence and easiest to find
confident advice.

## Technique: coaching consensus, graded as such

Everything in this section is **ASSERTED**. A targeted search for controlled work linking any of
these cues to reduced quadriceps damage or faster descending in trail runners returned nothing.
They are near-universal among coaches, they are mechanically plausible, and they are untested.

| Cue | The mechanical argument |
| --- | ----------------------- |
| **Quick, short steps** — raise cadence, shorten stride | A long stride downhill lands the foot well ahead of the hips on a near-straight leg, which is a brake. More steps spread the same descent across more, smaller braking impulses |
| **Land under the hips**, not in front | Foot ahead of the centre of mass is the braking position by definition |
| **Lean with the slope**, slightly forward from the ankles | The instinct is to lean back, which puts the weight on the heels and maximises braking. Leaning back is the single most common fault |
| **Look 3–5 m ahead**, not at the feet | Line choice has to precede the step. Watching your feet means reacting after the ground has arrived |
| **Stay relaxed** — loose hands, loose shoulders | Co-contraction costs energy and slows reaction |
| **Stop braking continuously** | Continuous braking is what destroys quadriceps. Controlled falling with fast feet is the alternative |
| **Use the whole trail** | Pick a line across the width available rather than following the fall line |

The strength side of the same problem is [[strength-training-for-ultrarunners]]; the course this
page exists for is [[hellbender-100]].

**The honest summary of the technique half:** it is the most likely explanation for large
descending time losses in runners who lack mountain experience, it is highly trainable, and its
evidence base is coaching opinion. Both halves of that sentence should be said together.

## The practical protocol

### Exposure

1. **Frequency over volume.** A short descent block regularly beats a mountain weekend
   occasionally — that is what the single-bout finding implies.
2. **Start before you think you need to.** The protection from one bout takes effect over the
   following two weeks, so the first exposure wants to be months out, not weeks.
3. **Practise tired.** Races are not lost descending on fresh legs at mile five. If descent work
   only ever happens fresh, it trains a condition the race never presents.
4. **Progress the technicality, not just the grade.** Smooth descent and root-and-rock descent are
   different skills and the second is the one that costs time.
5. **Stop the block when the lowering gets sloppy.** Descending practised badly while fatigued
   trains bad descending, and it is also when ankles get turned.

### Where to find descent without mountains

Ranked, for flat-country athletes — and note that **a treadmill cannot do this**, since almost none
decline:

1. **Multi-storey car park ramps.** Repeatable, continuous, weather-proof, and you can take the
   stairs or lift back up so that nearly all of the session is descending. The best flat-land
   option by a distance.
2. **Highway overpasses and bridge approaches.** Short, but they exist almost everywhere.
3. **Levees, spoil heaps, dunes, stadium ramps.**

See [[vertical-gain-and-its-substitutes]] for the ascent side of the same problem, and note the
asymmetry: ascent has decent indoor substitutes, descent has almost none.

## The flat sections, which are the other half of the lost time

A mountain hundred's **runnable** terrain is where time is cheapest to make, because climbs are
capped by sustainable power and steep descents by skill and quadriceps. Two opposite errors both
cost a cutoff-limited runner:

1. **Shuffling the runnable ground**, usually because an early climb has already been run too
   hard, or out of a vague belief that "ultras are hiked". This loses minutes per mile on precisely
   the terrain where speed is physiologically cheapest.
2. **Hammering the runnable ground early**, arriving at the climbs depleted — which then causes
   error 1 later, at the point in the race where the cutoffs tighten.

The resolution is not a pace. It is a rule: **run everything runnable, at an effort you would
describe as easy, from the start — and hike the climbs early enough that nothing has to be
walked later that could have been run.** The discipline is in the climbs, and it shows up as a
dividend on the flats.

## What this page does not claim

- That technique work has been shown to reduce damage. It has not been tested.
- That eccentric conditioning preserves descending pace deep in a race. The fatigue data say
  otherwise, or at least do not say so.
- That laboratory treadmill descent at -10% resembles a technical mountain trail. It does not.
