# The carbohydrate fuelling ladder

## What question it answers

*How many grams an hour should I take in this race, and how did I arrive at that number?*

## The ladder

Not one recommendation but a ladder, because the right rung depends on duration, intensity, and what
the athlete's gut has actually been trained to take.

| Rung | g/h | When | Standing |
| ---- | --- | ---- | -------- |
| Untrained default | 30–45 | An athlete who has never fuelled deliberately. The starting point of a gut-training block, not a race plan | `experienced` |
| Standard guidance | 60–90 | Events over ~2.5 h. The mainstream recommendation, with a glucose:fructose blend above 60 | `established` |
| Upper rung | ~120 | Elite, gut-trained, high-intensity, shorter-duration mountain racing. One trial | `established` for that population, `asserted` for anyone else |

**The number everyone quotes is 90, and increasingly 120.** Both are ceilings observed in specific
populations under specific conditions, and both travel as though they were targets for everyone. The
120 g/h result — lower muscle-damage markers 24 h post-race versus 90 and 60 g/h — is recorded with
its full caveats in [[carbohydrate-120g-mountain-marathon-trial]]. It is a real result in twenty
elite men who had already trained their guts for it. It is not a recommendation for a fourteen-hour
100k.

## The countervailing fact

Intake is not the constraint that binds first. **GI distress is the most commonly cited primary
reason for withdrawal** in the ultra DNF data recorded in [[gi-distress-and-dnf-figures]], and the
mechanism runs the wrong way for aggressive fuelling: distress reduces intake, and reduced intake
produces the deficit the aggressive plan was designed to avoid. A plan that maximises grams on paper
and produces nausea at hour six delivers fewer total grams than a conservative plan that is
tolerated.

**So the objective is not the highest rate. It is the highest rate that survives the whole race.**

## Inputs

- Expected duration and intensity. Intensity matters more than duration for tolerance: the higher
  the effort, the less blood the gut gets.
- Expected heat — see [[pacing-by-effort]] for the interaction, which is compounding rather than
  additive.
- What the athlete has *actually* tolerated in training, in grams, recorded. Not what they intend.
- Whether real food is planned. Above about 60 g/h, a mixed real-food strategy is hard to hit
  reliably and hard to compute.

## Steps

1. **Set the target from the bottom.** Start with what has been tolerated, not with 90.
2. **Raise it in training, not in the race** — [[gut-training-protocol]] is the mechanism, and it
   takes weeks.
3. **Blend above 60 g/h.** Below it, the single-pathway capacity is not saturated and a blend adds
   nothing but cost.
4. **Compute in grams of carbohydrate**, per product, and write the plan as a per-hour list of named
   items. A plan in "gels" is not a plan.
5. **Build in a fallback ladder.** Every plan needs a documented answer to "what do I take when I
   cannot take this", stepping down through: same rate different form → lower rate → liquid only →
   settle and rebuild. Most race plans have a target and no fallback, and the fallback is the part
   that gets used.
6. **Keep it separable from fluid.** Fuelling and drinking are different decisions with different
   signals — see [[drink-to-thirst]]. Tying carbohydrate to a drink mix couples them and makes both
   worse in heat.

## Output

An hourly plan in named products and grams, with a fallback ladder, tested at race intensity and
race duration-proxy at least twice.

## How to tell it is being applied wrongly

- **The target came from a podcast rather than from a training session.** The commonest failure.
- **90 g/h is treated as a floor.** It is a mainstream ceiling for trained athletes in shorter,
  harder events.
- **The plan has no fallback.** See above.
- **Grams are counted in products.** A "gel an hour" varies by a factor of two across brands.
- **It is used to fix an energy problem that is actually a daily-diet problem.** Arriving at the
  start line under-fuelled is a training-block failure, not a race-fuelling one — see
  [[energy-availability-floor]].
