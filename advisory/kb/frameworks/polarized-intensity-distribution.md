# Polarized intensity distribution

## What question it answers

*Of the hours I have, how many should be easy and how many hard, and where does the middle go?*

## The claim

Training intensity distribution (TID) is described in three zones: below the first ventilatory or
lactate threshold (Z1, conversational), between the two thresholds (Z2, the "moderate" or tempo
region), and above the second threshold (Z3, hard intervals). A **polarised** distribution puts
roughly 75–80% of work in Z1, 15–20% in Z3, and very little — around 5% — in Z2. The popular
shorthand is "80/20".

Two distinct claims travel under that name and they need separating, because only one of them is
well supported:

| Claim | Standing |
| ----- | -------- |
| **A.** Most endurance training should be easy — the large majority, not merely the plurality | Strong. Both observational and interventional evidence, consistent across sports |
| **B.** *Polarised* specifically beats *pyramidal* (a large Z1 base with more Z2 than Z3) and threshold-focused models | Weak. Meta-analysis finds no reliable separation; see [[polarized-training-review-evidence]] |

Most of what the sport calls "80/20" is claim A wearing claim B's clothes.

## Inputs

- A defensible Z1/Z2 boundary. Field-testable options: talk test, nose breathing, a lactate or
  ventilatory threshold test, or a heart-rate ceiling from a step protocol. Not a guess.
- Total weekly hours actually available — see [[minimum-effective-dose-and-time]], because the
  distribution question is downstream of the volume question and is frequently asked first.
- Whether the athlete is currently injured, ill, or under-fuelled. All three change the answer.

## Steps

1. **Fix the easy ceiling and hold it.** The single highest-value action is making easy days easy
   enough that they are recoverable. Most self-coached runners' problem is not too little Z3, it is
   that their Z1 is quietly Z2.
2. **Count by time, and say so.** Record the unit. A distribution reported without its unit is not
   a measurement.
3. **Place hard work where it survives.** One or two Z3 sessions a week is the working range for a
   6–10 hour athlete; a third is where the injury and adherence risk climbs steeply.
4. **Let the long run break the model deliberately.** In ultra training the long run is the session
   that most resembles the race, and its intensity is Z1 by heart rate and considerably more than
   Z1 by muscular and metabolic cost. Classifying it as "easy" and adding hard sessions around it
   on that basis is the commonest way a polarised plan becomes an overreaching one.

## Output

A weekly distribution stated in hours, by zone, with the boundary method named — not a ratio.

## How to tell it is being applied wrongly

- **The middle is treated as forbidden rather than expensive.** Z2 is not toxic. In a polarised
  model it is simply low-yield per unit of fatigue. For an ultrarunner whose race is run at an
  intensity somewhere near the bottom of Z2, a plan containing almost no Z2 has removed the most
  specific work in it. This is the sharpest limitation of importing a middle-distance finding into
  this sport.
- **The percentages are treated as measurements of what elites do.** The observational studies
  report what a training log said, on a three-zone scheme the athlete did not necessarily use.
- **It is applied to an athlete with six hours a week.** The polarised evidence comes from athletes
  training two to four times that. At six hours, the argument for spending 80% of them easy is
  materially weaker and is not what the studies tested. This is a live disagreement between
  coaching schools rather than a settled question — see
  [[coaching-schools-and-where-they-disagree]].
- **The distribution is optimised before the volume is stable.** Ratio work on a block that keeps
  not happening is the most common way this framework wastes attention.
