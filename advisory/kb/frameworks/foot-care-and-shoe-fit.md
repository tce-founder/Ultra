# Foot care and shoe fit

> **Medical boundary.** Friction injury that is not resolving, any posterior heel swelling that
> persists past a couple of weeks off, numbness, or a bony prominence at the back of the heel
> (which can predispose to bursitis) is a question for a physician or podiatrist, not for this
> page. What follows is prevention and management, not diagnosis.

## Why this page ranks higher in this sport than its evidence does

Feet end races. Not often in the dramatic way GI distress does, but steadily: a blister at mile 30
changes gait, an altered gait loads something else, and the something else is what stops you.
Almost none of it is in the literature, and nearly all of it is preventable by decisions made
weeks earlier in a shop rather than hours earlier at an aid station.

## 1. The posterior heel — the one the vert plan can cause

**The mechanism.** Steep climbing holds the ankle in **dorsiflexion**. In dorsiflexion the
calcaneus rotates and presses backward into the shoe's **heel counter**. Add repetition, a stiff
or high counter, a seam, or a heel that pistons because the lacing is loose, and the result is
friction over the retrocalcaneal and superficial calcaneal bursae — inflammation at the back of
the heel, above the insertion of the Achilles.

**Why this matters more than it looks.** It is the one injury in this corpus whose cause is
*equipment* rather than *load*. That has two consequences that run in opposite directions:

- **Good news:** rest plus removing the offending shoe genuinely resolves it. There is no deep
  biomechanical fault to rehabilitate, so "it settled on its own" is an adequate outcome here in a
  way it would not be for, say, trochanteric bursitis from hip-abductor weakness.
- **Bad news:** nothing about the resolution reduces the risk of recurrence. The cause is still
  sitting in the shoe rack, and the single most provocative training session anyone can prescribe
  for it is **sustained steep climbing** — a stair machine, a treadmill at maximum incline, or a
  mountain.

**So a mountain-ultra plan is, for this specific injury, a loaded gun.** It prescribes weekly
dorsiflexion under load for months. The mitigation is not to drop the vert; it is to fix the shoe
and then watch the heel.

### What to do

1. **Audit the rack.** Press a thumb into the back of every heel counter. Stiff, tall, or seamed
   over the Achilles → out of the rotation. This is the whole intervention and it takes ten minutes.
2. **Prefer a soft, low collar** for climbing sessions and for the race.
3. **Lock the heel.** Use the top eyelet properly so the heel does not piston against the counter.
   A heel that moves rubs; a heel that is held does not. This matters more than the counter's
   stiffness.
4. **Consider drop.** A higher heel-to-toe drop reduces the dorsiflexion range the ankle has to
   reach, which reduces counter contact. This is a trade — lower drop has other arguments for it —
   but on a 21,000 ft course the climbing argument is the live one.
5. **Load the tendon on flat ground first.** Calf raises are protective for the Achilles and should
   be in the plan. Doing them hanging off a step, in loaded end-range dorsiflexion, is the same
   provocation as the hill. Flat ground until symptom-free under climbing load.
6. **Stop at the first sign, not the second.** This injury's whole character is that it is cheap
   to interrupt and expensive to ignore. One easy week early beats seven months off.

### The substitution ladder, if it niggles

Keep the aerobic stimulus, drop the provocation:

| Rung | Swap | Keeps | Loses |
| ---- | ---- | ----- | ----- |
| 1 | Uphill treadmill **walking**, higher-drop shoe | Most of the climbing demand | Some cadence specificity |
| 2 | Weighted pack hike on moderate grade | Load and time on feet | Steepness |
| 3 | Bike or elliptical for the aerobic half | Aerobic stimulus | All climbing specificity |

Rung 3 is not a failure. A month on the bike with a healthy heel beats a month of climbing on a
heel that is quietly inflaming.

## 2. Blisters and maceration

The ordinary ultra foot problem, and mostly a moisture-and-friction problem rather than a
toughness one.

- **Size for swelling.** Feet swell over hours and over climbing; a shoe that fits at mile one can
  be the injury at mile forty. Half a size up over race distance is common practice.
- **Manage moisture before friction.** Wet skin blisters at a fraction of the load dry skin does.
  Change socks at planned points, not when it already hurts.
- **Lubricant or powder, pick one and test it.** Both work for some people; mixing them makes paste.
- **Tape known hot spots prophylactically**, before the run, on skin that is clean and dry.
- **Toenails short and square** a few days before — not the night before, which leaves tender beds.
- **Creek crossings are a decision, not an accident.** Know where they are and whether you are
  changing socks after.

## 3. The race-day kit this implies

- Two or more sock changes in drop bags, at known points.
- A second pair of shoes at a crew point, half a size up, for the late swelling.
- Tape, lubricant and a blister kit you have actually used in training.
- **Nothing new on race day**, which is the one rule here that is not negotiable.

## How to tell this is being applied wrongly

- **Foot care is treated as an aid-station activity.** It is a shoe-shop and training-block
  activity; by the aid station the options are all bad.
- **The shoe that caused a problem is kept "for easy runs".** Friction does not care about pace.
- **Posterior heel soreness is trained through** because it is "just a niggle". It is the early,
  cheap phase of the thing that costs months.
- **A substitution is treated as lost fitness.** See the ladder above — see also
  [[vertical-gain-and-its-substitutes]], which covers what indoor climbing does and does not buy,
  and [[the-dnf-decision]], where an unmanaged foot becomes a row-one stop.
- **Race-day kit is untested.** Everything on the list above gets used on a mountain weekend
  first, including on the [[hellbender-100]] course if possible.
