# Pacing by effort

## What question it answers

*What am I holding constant over the next fourteen hours?*

## The claim

In a marathon the answer is pace, and the literature on negative splitting is about how to hold or
slightly improve it. **In a mountain ultra, pace is not a controllable variable and holding it is a
mistake.** The gradient changes the cost of a kilometre by a factor of three or more, so a runner
holding pace uphill is racing and a runner holding pace downhill is coasting.

The transferable variable is **effort** — subjectively rated, held deliberately low early, and
explicitly *not* heart rate, which drifts upward at a fixed effort over hours and will drag an
athlete slower and slower if chased.

Observations from mountain-ultra race data, offered at their real standing (`experienced`, not
`established`):

- Faster finishers show **lower variability of speed**, a **smaller late-race slowdown**, and **less
  aid-station dead time**.
- **Positive splits are normal**, even for winners. A slowdown of roughly 10–20% in the second half
  is unremarkable on a mountain course.
- On flatter, faster ultras, pacing can move back toward even or mildly negative.

Note the survivorship problem before acting on the first bullet: fast finishers differ from the rest
of the field in fitness, experience and everything else, so their pacing pattern is a correlate of
being fast, not a proven cause of it.

## Inputs

- The course profile — vertical gain, where the climbs are, and critically **where the descents
  are**, since that is where the damage accumulates.
- Cutoffs, which are the hard constraint an effort-based plan must still satisfy.
- Expected heat, which shifts the whole effort–pace relationship — see
  [[heat-acclimation-protocol]].
- The fuelling plan, because effort and gut tolerance are coupled — see
  [[carbohydrate-fueling-ladder]].

## Steps

1. **Set an effort ceiling for the first third and defend it.** The commonest expensive error in the
   sport is a first climb run at an effort that felt easy and was not.
2. **Pace the descents by damage, not by speed.** Descending is cheap metabolically and expensive
   structurally. The first descent is where the last thirty kilometres are decided, and it is the
   demand no indoor training substitutes for — see [[vertical-gain-and-its-substitutes]].
3. **Hike the climbs deliberately, on a rule.** A gradient threshold or a heart-rate ceiling above
   which you always hike, set before the race and not renegotiated on the day.
4. **Count aid-station time.** It is the cheapest minutes in the race and the least defended. A plan
   with a per-stop time budget and a written list of what happens at each is worth more than most
   training interventions.
5. **Re-anchor effort as conditions change.** Heat, night and altitude all move the effort/pace
   relationship. The effort stays constant; the pace it buys does not, and that is the plan working.
6. **Do not chase heart rate.** Cardiac drift makes it a decreasing function of the thing you care
   about.

## Output

A pacing plan expressed as effort ceilings by segment, hike/run gradient rules, per-aid-station time
budgets, and a stated expected slowdown — not a split table.

## How to tell it is being applied wrongly

- **A split table exists and is being defended.** On a mountain course it encodes an assumption
  about terrain that the terrain will not honour.
- **Heart rate is the pacing variable.** It drifts.
- **The descents are "free".** They are the most expensive part of the course in the currency that
  actually runs out.
- **Effort is re-rated upward as the race goes on.** Perceived effort rises at constant work; the
  discipline is to hold the *rating*, which means slowing.
- **The plan has no relationship to the fuelling plan or the night plan.** Going out too hard
  reduces gut blood flow, which produces the distress recorded in [[gi-distress-and-dnf-figures]],
  which produces the deficit, which produces the decision described in [[the-dnf-decision]] — and by
  then it is being made at 3 a.m. by someone who has not slept, per [[sleep-and-the-night]]. That
  chain is the single most common way an ultra ends, and it starts at hour one.
- **The plan was never rehearsed.** See [[periodization-for-ultras]] on the specific block.
