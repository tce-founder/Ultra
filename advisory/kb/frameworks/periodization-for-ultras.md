# Periodisation for ultras

## What question it answers

*I have N weeks until the race. In what order should the training happen, and what is the phase I am
in now for?*

## Why the ordinary answer imports badly

Classical periodisation was built for events shorter than a single training session, where the race
intensity is high and the problem is peaking. In an ultra the race is longer than anything in the
plan, the intensity is low, and the limiting factors are durability, fuelling and decision-making
rather than peak power. Three consequences:

1. **There is no meaningful "peak".** What is being built is tolerance, not sharpness. The taper is
   a recovery device, not a supercompensation device.
2. **Specificity is unreachable by duration.** No amateur can rehearse a 100 miler. Specificity is
   bought in *other dimensions* — terrain, vertical gain, time on feet, fuelling rate, sleep
   deprivation, night, heat — one or two at a time.
3. **The last hard block is the one that most often causes the injury.** The pattern is
   well-known to coaches and unmeasured: the athlete, sensing the race approaching, compresses the
   remaining specific work.

## The shape most schools agree on

| Phase | Length | The one thing it is for | Failure if skipped |
| ----- | ------ | ----------------------- | ------------------ |
| **Base** | 8–16 weeks | Aerobic volume at a recoverable intensity; connective-tissue tolerance | Everything later is built on a structure that cannot absorb it |
| **Strength / durability** | Overlaps base, 8+ weeks | Load the tissue deliberately rather than accidentally — see [[strength-training-for-ultrarunners]] | Late-race economy collapse and eccentric damage on descents |
| **Specific** | 6–10 weeks | The race's actual demands, added one at a time: terrain, vert, fuelling rate, heat, night | The athlete arrives fit and unrehearsed, which is the profile of a fuelling DNF |
| **Taper** | 1–3 weeks | Arrive not-tired. Volume falls, some intensity stays | Fresh legs are worth more than the last long run; this is the phase amateurs cut |

Phase lengths vary by school and by athlete more than any table can carry. Treat the *order* as the
transferable content and the numbers as conventions.

## Inputs

- Race date, distance, vertical gain, expected finish time, expected conditions, cutoffs.
- Weeks available, and honest weekly hours — [[minimum-effective-dose-and-time]] is the constraint
  this plan is built inside, not a footnote to it.
- The athlete's injury history, because it decides how fast volume may rise.
- The last block that actually happened, not the last block that was written.

## Steps

1. **Count backwards from the race**, not forwards from today. Place the taper first, then the
   specific block, then whatever is left is base.
2. **Name each phase's single purpose.** A phase with two purposes is a phase that will deliver
   neither when the week goes wrong.
3. **Rehearse in dimensions, not in distance.** In the specific block, one long session per week
   should carry a race-realistic version of exactly one thing: the descent, the fuelling rate, the
   night start, the heat, the pack. Stacking all of them is a race, not a session.
4. **Put a recovery week in and then actually take it.** The commonest amendment made mid-block is
   deleting the down week, and it is the amendment that most reliably ends the block.
5. **Re-plan on what happened.** A plan that has not been rewritten by week six was not being read.

## Output

A phase map with dates, one stated purpose per phase, the specific-block rehearsal list, and a named
condition under which the plan gets cut rather than compressed.

## How to tell it is being applied wrongly

- **The plan survives contact unchanged.** Illness, work and weather happen; a plan that never
  changes is a plan nobody is comparing to reality.
- **Compression instead of subtraction.** Four missed weeks means dropping goals, not fitting four
  weeks of work into two. This is where the sport's injuries are made.
- **The taper is treated as optional.** It is the cheapest performance in the plan and the first cut.
- **Specificity is defined as "long".** A six-hour flat road run does not prepare a descent — see
  [[pacing-by-effort]] for why the race demand is muscular and not only metabolic.
- **The vertical-gain phase is planned in kilometres.** Use metres climbed.

## Standing of this page

`authority: experienced`. This is coaching convention, drawn from coaching organisations' public
material, and it is offered as convention. There is no periodisation trial in ultrarunning that
would let any of it be stated as `established`, and the honest position is that the *order* is well
agreed across schools that disagree about almost everything else, which is itself weak evidence that
the order is right.
