# Heat acclimation

## What question it answers

*The race will be hot. What can I actually change about that, starting when, and what happens if I
stop?*

## The adaptations, and their clocks

Heat acclimation is one of the few interventions in this corpus with a genuine, measurable, fast
effect. It is also one where the timeline is routinely misstated in both directions.

| Adaptation | Roughly when | Notes |
| ---------- | ------------ | ----- |
| Plasma volume expansion | First week | Reported around 7% after five days in one endurance-athlete protocol |
| Lower resting and exercising heart rate | First week | The most noticeable subjective change |
| Increased sweat rate, earlier sweat onset | 1–2 weeks | Increases fluid losses — an adaptation with a cost |
| Lower sweat sodium concentration | Slower, ~2 weeks | Partly why sodium plans built pre-acclimation over-correct |
| Robust performance improvement | ~9 days and beyond | Four days was insufficient in a female cohort where nine days worked |

The figures and their sources are recorded in [[heat-acclimation-figures]].

**The two errors.** Underestimating: "a few hot runs" is not a protocol; the performance effects
cluster past a week of consecutive exposure. Overestimating: the adaptations **decay**, on a scale
of days to a couple of weeks without exposure, so a block finished a month out has largely gone.

## Inputs

- Race date, expected temperature and **humidity** — humidity is the variable that decides whether
  sweat does anything, and it is the one athletes leave out.
- 10–14 consecutive days available, ending close to the race but not colliding with the taper.
- A heat source that can be used consistently: a chamber, a hot room, a treadmill in extra layers, a
  hot bath or sauna after training. Consistency matters more than sophistication.
- A way of not making the athlete ill. This is a real load and it is additive to training stress.

## Steps

1. **Place the block to finish within about a week of the race.** Adaptation decays; finishing three
   weeks out wastes most of it.
2. **Aim for consecutive days.** Ten to fourteen. Gaps cost more than the protocol's intensity does.
3. **Target the physiological signal, not the clock.** The laboratory method holds core temperature
   near 38.5 °C; the field proxy is sustained, uncomfortable, sweating heat stress for roughly
   60–90 minutes, at a low running intensity or passively after training.
4. **Reduce running intensity while acclimating.** The heat is the stimulus. Adding intensity to it
   produces a hard block, not an acclimation block, and this is where the illness happens.
5. **Drink to thirst throughout** — see [[drink-to-thirst]]. Acclimation increases sweat rate, which
   tempts a scheduled-drinking response, which is the mechanism to avoid.
6. **Re-anchor pacing afterwards.** Heart rate at a given effort falls; if pace targets were set
   pre-block they are now wrong in the useful direction and should be re-derived rather than
   assumed — see [[pacing-by-effort]].
7. **Top up if there is a gap.** Two or three sessions a week between the block and the race
   preserve most of it.

## Output

A dated block, a per-session record of exposure duration and subjective heat strain, and a re-derived
effort/heart-rate anchor afterwards.

## How to tell it is being applied wrongly

- **It is done once and counted.** Non-consecutive sessions are not a protocol.
- **It is done four months out, "to be safe".** Decayed.
- **The humidity of the target race is unknown.** Dry heat and humid heat are different problems and
  the second one is the dangerous one, because evaporative cooling fails.
- **A sauna protocol is credited with the treadmill protocol's evidence.** Passive and active
  acclimation are not identical and the amateur evidence base is thinner than the laboratory one.
- **It is treated as a substitute for pacing conservatively in heat.** It reduces the penalty; it
  does not remove it.
- **The athlete is female and the protocol was sized from male data.** The four-versus-nine-day
  result is the standing reason to plan the longer block.
