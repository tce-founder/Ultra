# Drinking and sodium

> **Medical boundary.** Exercise-associated hyponatremia can be fatal and its early symptoms —
> nausea, headache, confusion, malaise — are indistinguishable from ordinary late-race misery. This
> page produces questions for a clinician and a plan for avoiding the mechanism. It does not
> diagnose and it does not treat. Suspected hyponatremia in the field is a medical emergency, not a
> pacing decision.

## What question it answers

*How much should I drink, on what signal, and does sodium supplementation do anything?*

## The two positions, and where they actually conflict

**Consensus position.** The Third International Exercise-Associated Hyponatremia Consensus
Development Conference concluded that the primary mechanism of EAH is **overconsumption of hypotonic
fluid**, in combination with non-osmotic vasopressin secretion, and recommends that athletes **drink
according to thirst — no more, no less**. Drinking beyond thirst is the primary pathophysiological
factor regardless of the activity.

**Field finding on sodium.** In a hot-weather ultramarathon field study, exercise-associated muscle
cramping, dehydration, hyponatremia and nausea/vomiting were **unrelated to total sodium intake**,
despite the belief among ultrarunners that sodium prevents them. Recorded with its numbers and its
limits in [[sodium-does-not-prevent-cramping]].

**Commercial position.** Sweat sodium concentration varies widely between individuals, therefore
sodium replacement should be personalised and, for high-sweat-sodium athletes, substantial. This is
not straightforwardly false — the inter-individual variation is real and measurable — but the party
publishing it sells the tests and the products, which is recorded in
[[fueling-brands-and-their-interests]] rather than treated as disqualifying.

**Where they actually conflict** is narrower than the argument suggests. Nobody disputes that sodium
losses vary. What is disputed is whether replacing them *prevents anything*, and on that the field
evidence is null and the consensus mechanism points at fluid volume rather than sodium content.

## Inputs

- Expected duration, heat and humidity.
- The athlete's history: any previous swelling, any previous end-of-race weight *gain*, any episode
  of confusion or disproportionate nausea. All three are risk signals and all three belong in front
  of a clinician before the race, not after it.
- Whether fluid and carbohydrate are coupled in the same product — see below.

## Steps

1. **Drink to thirst.** This is the recommendation, and its main practical difficulty is that it
   contradicts a decade of "stay ahead of your thirst" messaging that many athletes still carry.
2. **Do not drink on a schedule.** A fixed ml/h target ignores the only signal that tracks the
   actual state, and scheduled drinking is the behaviour the consensus identifies as the mechanism
   of harm.
3. **Weigh in and out in training** where practical. Large weight *gain* over a long session is the
   clearest available warning that intake is exceeding losses. This is a training-block measurement,
   not a race-day one.
4. **Decouple fluid from fuel.** If carbohydrate arrives only in a drink mix, then in heat the
   athlete must drink to fuel and in cold must fuel to drink. Carry at least some carbohydrate in a
   form that does not require fluid — see [[carbohydrate-fueling-ladder]].
5. **Treat sodium as comfort and palatability, not as prophylaxis.** Salty food at an aid station is
   welcome and probably useful for appetite. Framing it as preventing cramp is a claim the evidence
   does not support.
6. **Escalate rather than manage.** Nausea plus headache plus disorientation, especially with high
   fluid intake and no weight loss, is a medical question. Stop and get it looked at.

## Output

A fluid plan whose signal is thirst, a carbohydrate plan that does not depend on fluid volume, and a
written escalation trigger.

## How to tell it is being applied wrongly

- **A litre-per-hour target exists.** That is the mechanism of the thing being avoided.
- **Salt tablets are the answer to cramp.** The field evidence says no; cramp's causes are more
  likely neuromuscular fatigue and pacing.
- **Dehydration is treated as the only risk.** Both directions kill; only one of them is famous.
- **Heat acclimation is skipped because sodium is being supplemented.** They are not substitutes —
  see [[heat-acclimation-protocol]].
