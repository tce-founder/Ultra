# Gut training

## What question it answers

*My race plan needs 80 g/h and 50 g/h makes me feel sick. Is that fixed, and how long does fixing it
take?*

## The claim

Intestinal carbohydrate absorption is **adaptable**. Repeated exposure to high carbohydrate intake
during exercise upregulates transporter capacity and gastric tolerance, and the literature suggests
meaningful adaptation in as little as two weeks. It is the reason the 120 g/h trial recorded in
[[carbohydrate-120g-mountain-marathon-trial]] could be run at all: its subjects had already been
through a gut-training programme, and that precondition is part of the result rather than context to
it.

The corollary is the operative one: **an athlete's current tolerance is a training state, not a
fixed trait.** "I have a sensitive stomach" is, in a large fraction of cases, a description of
someone who has never trained the organ they are describing.

## Inputs

- The target rate from [[carbohydrate-fueling-ladder]], and the current tolerated rate, both in
  grams per hour and both actually measured.
- Six to twelve weeks before the race. This belongs in the specific block, not the taper.
- The exact products intended for race day. Adaptation is partly product-specific — osmolality,
  blend ratio and form all differ, and a gut trained on one drink mix has not been trained on gels.

## Steps

1. **Measure the baseline honestly.** One long session at race intensity, fuelling at the current
   habitual rate, with the grams written down and the symptoms recorded.
2. **Raise on the long run only.** Roughly +10 g/h per week, on the one weekly session that most
   resembles the race. Not on easy runs, where the gut has plenty of blood flow and learns nothing.
3. **Hold the intensity race-realistic.** Tolerance is intensity-dependent. Fuelling at 90 g/h on a
   flat jog proves nothing about hour nine of a mountain race.
4. **Change one variable at a time.** Rate, product, form, temperature, and concentration are five
   variables. Moving two at once means a symptom teaches nothing.
5. **Rehearse the fallback ladder too.** Practise the step-down, not only the target. It is the part
   that gets used.
6. **Treat the pre-race state as part of the protocol.** Pre-race anxiety correlates with GI
   symptoms; the practical consequence is that a start-line routine is a fuelling intervention as
   well as a psychological one.

## Output

A recorded progression — week, session, rate, product, symptoms — ending at a rate demonstrated
twice at race intensity. The record is the deliverable; without it the next block starts from
memory.

## How to tell it is being applied wrongly

- **It is attempted in the taper.** Too late to adapt and early enough to ruin the race.
- **The race products are not the training products.** Extremely common and it discards most of the
  specificity.
- **Symptoms are pushed through rather than stepped back from.** The protocol is progressive
  overload, not a test of will. A session that ends in vomiting has produced a week of avoidance,
  not an adaptation.
- **It is used to rescue a plan whose real problem is pace.** Blood flow to the gut falls with
  intensity; an athlete who goes out too hard will not tolerate a rate they tolerate at correct
  effort, and the fix is the pacing, not the gut. [[gi-distress-and-dnf-figures]] records how often
  the resulting distress ends the race.
- **A single bad session is read as a fixed trait.** One data point in an adaptable system.
