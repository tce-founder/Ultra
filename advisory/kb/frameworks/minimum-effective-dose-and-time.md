# The minimum effective dose

## What question it answers

*I have a job and a family and maybe eight hours. Is that enough, and what should be in them?*

## The claim, and its honest standing

Coaching sources converge on a working range of roughly **6–10 hours a week** being sufficient to
prepare an amateur for a long ultra, with the recurring assertion that **consistency dominates
volume** — the athlete who trains eight hours a week reliably beats the athlete who intends fifteen
and delivers nine of them irregularly.

This is repeated everywhere and measured nowhere. It is `experienced`, from sources that
substantially sell plans to time-crunched athletes, and it is recorded as such in
[[time-crunched-volume-claims]]. It is also, in the judgement of this corpus, probably true — but
"probably true and unmeasured" is a different thing from "established", and the distinction is
exactly what this schema exists to preserve.

## Why this lens ranks higher here than its evidence would suggest

Almost all of this corpus is about optimising training that happens. This page is about whether it
happens, and for an amateur that is the larger term by a wide margin. A perfect periodisation
executed at 60% completion loses to an ordinary one executed at 95%. Nothing else in the corpus has
that leverage, and nothing else in the corpus has this little evidence behind it — a mismatch worth
stating out loud rather than resolving by pretending one of the two is different.

## Inputs

- **The real number of hours**, arrived at by looking at the last twelve weeks rather than by
  planning the next one. Athletes overestimate available hours by a large and consistent margin.
- Fixed constraints: work pattern, childcare, commute, the days that are simply not available.
- Total life load. Occupational stress and training stress draw on the same recovery, and a high-
  stress work period reduces training capacity — which means the correct response to a hard quarter
  at work is to train less, not to defend the plan.
- Sleep. An early alarm that costs an hour's sleep to gain an hour's running is frequently a net
  loss; that trade belongs in this budget.

## Steps

1. **Budget from observed hours, not intended ones.** Then subtract 15% for illness, weather and
   life, and plan against what is left. A plan built on the optimistic number fails weekly, and
   weekly failure is how adherence dies.
2. **Protect one long session absolutely.** In an eight-hour week the long run is a third to a half
   of the total and carries most of the specificity. Everything else is negotiable.
3. **Make the rest structurally cheap.** Sessions that need travel, daylight, a training partner or
   good weather have four failure points. Sessions from the door, on a treadmill, or before anyone
   else is awake have one. Reliability is a design property, not a character trait — see
   [[vertical-gain-and-its-substitutes]] on what the indoor options can and cannot carry.
4. **Decide the cut order in advance.** When the week collapses, what goes first? Write it down.
   This is the question every source dodges, and deciding it in advance is the difference between
   a reduced week and an abandoned one.
5. **Track completion rate, not volume.** Percentage of planned sessions done over rolling twelve
   weeks. It is the number that predicts the race and the only one this page can be judged by.
6. **When hours fall below the fuelling floor, cut hours.** Under-fuelled training is worse than
   absent training — see [[energy-availability-floor]]. This is the one hard constraint here.
7. **Re-scope the goal rather than compressing the plan.** See [[periodization-for-ultras]]. A race
   the available hours cannot support should be changed, and changing it is a legitimate outcome of
   this lens rather than a failure of it.

## Output

A weekly budget in hours against observed availability, a protected long session, a written cut
order, and a rolling completion-rate figure.

## How to tell it is being applied wrongly

- **The plan is built on intended hours.** The most common error, and it produces a plan that fails
  in week three and an athlete who concludes they lack discipline.
- **The cut order does not exist**, so the first collapsed week takes the long run with it.
- **Intensity is used to buy back volume without the recovery cost being counted.** Attractive,
  frequently sold, and where the time-crunched athlete gets injured.
- **Consistency is treated as a virtue rather than a design output.** It is produced by removing
  failure points, not by resolve. Where resolve genuinely is the variable, the material is in
  [[mental-skills-toolkit]] — but check the design first, because it usually is not.
