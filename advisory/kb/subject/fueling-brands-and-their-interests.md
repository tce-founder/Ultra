# Fuelling and hydration brands

## The position this page takes

**Some of the best fuelling education in endurance sport is published by companies selling fuel.**
That is not a paradox and it is not a reason to discount it — they employ the physiologists, they
fund some of the research, and their incentive to have athletes fuel successfully is real.

The rule is therefore **record the interest, then read the content**, and check the population before
acting. A brand's summary of a study is `citation_status: acquired` and cites the brand.

## The organisations

| Organisation | What they publish | Where their interest lies |
| ------------ | ----------------- | ------------------------- |
| **Precision Fuel & Hydration** | Detailed, well-written fuelling and hydration guidance; carbohydrate-per-hour material; the personalised sweat-sodium argument | **Sells sweat tests and electrolyte products.** Its central content claim — that sodium losses vary and should be personally replaced — is the claim its business is built on |
| **Science in Sport (SiS)** | Ultra fuelling guides, product-linked protocols | Sells gels, drinks and blends |
| **The Feed** | Aggregator and retailer; explainer content on high-rate fuelling | Retail. Its "90 g/h changes everything" framing sells more product per hour of racing |
| **Maurten, Precision, Neversecond and similar** | Blend-ratio and hydrogel formulations, each with a mechanistic story | Product differentiation; the mechanism is the marketing |
| **CTS / TrainRight** | Coaching rather than product, but the GI-distress and gut-training material used in this corpus reaches it through here | Sells coaching. Its interest runs toward "this is trainable and we can train you" |

## The one substantive contradiction

On sodium, the commercial position and the consensus position genuinely conflict.

**Commercial:** sweat sodium concentration varies several-fold between individuals; therefore
replacement should be personalised, and for high-sweat-sodium athletes substantial.

**Evidence:** in a hot-weather ultramarathon field study, cramping, dehydration, hyponatremia and
nausea were **unrelated** to total sodium intake; and the consensus position identifies
**over-drinking**, not sodium deficit, as the mechanism of exercise-associated hyponatremia. See
[[sodium-does-not-prevent-cramping]] and the practical position in [[drink-to-thirst]].

**What is actually in dispute is narrower than it looks.** Nobody disputes that sweat sodium varies.
What is disputed is whether replacing it *prevents anything*, and the field study is a null result
that could not detect an effect confined to the high-loss tail — which is precisely the group the
commercial argument is about. So the strongest form of the brands' case is not refuted by the
strongest form of the evidence. It is unaddressed.

Both are held in this corpus, with their evidence, rather than one being deleted. That is what a
`counterpoint` disposition is for.

## Where the brands are most useful

- **Product carbohydrate content in grams**, which is the number a plan is actually built from and
  which nobody else publishes.
- **Blend ratios and osmolality**, which matter above 60 g/h.
- **Practical progression guidance** for raising tolerated rate — see [[gut-training-protocol]].
- **Honest reporting of the ranges**, which is often better in brand content than in the media
  coverage of the same studies.

## Where to be most careful

- **Ceilings quoted as targets.** The 90 and 120 g/h figures are population-bound ceilings; see
  [[carbohydrate-fueling-ladder]] for the ladder that replaces them.
- **The population lost in one hop.** "Research shows" in a brand article almost never names who the
  research was done on.
- **Sodium framed as prophylaxis** rather than as palatability.
- **Gated protocols**, which become `ip_class: licensed` on ingest even though the free content
  around them is public.
