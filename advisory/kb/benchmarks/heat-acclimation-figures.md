# Heat acclimation figures

## The numbers

| Figure | What it is | Population |
| ------ | ---------- | ---------- |
| **~7.3%** | Plasma volume increase after **five days** of heat acclimation, alongside reduced resting heart rate and increased sweat loss | Endurance athletes, isothermic protocol |
| **32 °C, 70% RH, core ≥38.5 °C** | The five-day isothermic protocol's conditions | Same |
| **90 min at 40 °C, core ~38.5 °C** | The nine-day protocol's session | Endurance-trained |
| **Nine days yes, four days no** | Nine days of isothermic acclimation improved self-paced endurance performance in females; **four days did not** | **Females** — the one sex-specific result in this set |
| **7–14 days** | Where measurable improvements typically appear, with larger adaptations over several weeks | Across the literature |

## The result that changes a recommendation

The four-versus-nine-day finding in a female cohort is the most actionable item here, and it is
actionable precisely because it is the only study in the set that was not run on men. Much of the
sport's "a few days is enough" folklore traces to shorter male protocols. The standing consequence
in [[heat-acclimation-protocol]] is to plan the longer block by default rather than to treat five
days as sufficient.

This is worth stating as a general lesson about this corpus and not only about heat: **where a
sex-specific study exists in this literature, it has more often complicated the male finding than
confirmed it.** The `population` field is the mechanism that keeps that visible.

## Consistent across the set

- Plasma volume expansion occurs within the **first week** and is the earliest large adaptation.
- Heart-rate reduction and sweat-rate increase are early and reliable.
- Performance improvements are more robust from around **day nine** onward.
- Adaptations **decay** without continued exposure — which is why block placement matters more than
  block intensity.

## Provenance

Abstract-level reading of open-access papers plus one commercial commentary piece on measuring heat
adaptation. Full texts **not** read: `citation_status: acquired`. The specific risk on this page is
the plasma-volume unit — the 7.3% figure is meaningless without knowing it is a percentage of plasma
volume, and secondary reporting frequently drops that. Anyone promoting this page should re-derive
the figure and its denominator from the paper.

The absence of any evaluation of amateur field methods — sauna, hot bath, extra layers — is recorded
in [[where-the-evidence-lives]] and is, for a self-coached athlete, the gap that matters most here.
