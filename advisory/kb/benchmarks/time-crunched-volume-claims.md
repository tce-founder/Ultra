# Time-crunched volume claims

## The claims

| Claim | Source type |
| ----- | ----------- |
| 6–8 well-organised hours a week are enough to improve without unbalancing life | Coaching article |
| Many age-group athletes cap at **8–10 h/week** total; 8–10 is "ideal" for most single-sport athletes | Coaching article |
| Single-sport athletes racing under three hours improve meaningfully on **6–10 h/week** | Coaching article |
| **Consistency beats volume** — eight hours reliably beats fifteen intended and nine delivered | Repeated across all sources |
| Occupational stress and training stress draw on the same physiological resources; high work stress reduces training capacity | Coaching article |
| Three consistent days beats four attempted with partial completion | Coaching article |

## Standing: `experienced`, and thinly so

Every one of these is a practitioner assertion. None is a measurement. They are repeated across
sources, which is weaker evidence than it looks — these sources read each other, so agreement is
partly circulation rather than independent confirmation.

They are also **commercially interested**. The organisations publishing "you can do this on eight
hours" sell plans to people with eight hours. Again: not disqualifying. The claim is plausible, the
mechanism (adherence dominates) is sound, and the alternative advice on offer is worse. But
`authority: experienced` is the honest label and this page exists so that
[[minimum-effective-dose-and-time]] can quote the numbers without inheriting more confidence than
they carry.

## The gap, which is the largest in this corpus

**There is no measurement of the relationship between amateur training volume and ultramarathon
outcome.** Not finishing probability, not finish time, not injury rate, not dropout. The question
every amateur asks first — *is this enough?* — has no evidential answer anywhere consulted, in a
sport with tens of thousands of finishers a year whose training data is more comprehensively
recorded on commercial platforms than almost any other amateur activity in history.

The data exists. Nobody has published the analysis.

That is recorded as the leading item in [[where-the-evidence-lives]], and it is the one gap in this
corpus that a motivated person with access to a platform's data could actually close.

## What is genuinely transferable

Two things, and they do not depend on the numbers:

1. **Adherence is a design property.** Sessions with fewer failure points get done. That is a claim
   about session design, and it is testable by the athlete against their own completion rate.
2. **Work stress and training stress share a budget.** Which means a hard quarter at work is a reason
   to train less, not a reason to defend the plan harder.

Both are carried into [[minimum-effective-dose-and-time]] as the parts worth acting on, with the
hour figures held as conventions rather than targets.
