# REDs and low energy availability

> **Medical boundary.** This is a clinical syndrome. This page records the evidence so an advisory
> system knows when to stop and refer. It does not support diagnosis by anyone who is not a
> clinician, and no output drawing on it may imply otherwise.

## The consensus

Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs) is a syndrome of impaired health and performance arising
from **low energy availability relative to the demands of training**. The IOC published a consensus
statement in **September 2023**, following substantial advances in the underlying physiology and
psychology. Affected systems named include energy metabolism, reproductive function, bone health,
immune function, protein synthesis and cardiovascular health.

## Prevalence and risk

- Low energy availability is **common among female endurance athletes**, where the literature is
  deepest.
- It is also found at meaningful prevalence in **competitively trained male endurance athletes** —
  the finding that most changes practice, because the belief that this is a women's condition is
  itself a reason male athletes go unscreened.
- **Endurance sports** and aesthetic sports are named as higher risk than ball sports.
- Co-occurrence with disordered eating, exercise addiction and reported food intolerances is
  documented in female endurance athletes.

## The syndrome debate, and why it does not change the gate

There is published disagreement about whether REDs is best understood as a discrete syndrome at all,
including a paper asking the question in its title. That debate is about **construct validity** —
whether the cluster of findings is one entity with one mechanism.

It does not touch the operative fact. Low energy availability has demonstrable consequences for bone,
endocrine and immune function whether or not those consequences are best grouped under one name. The
gate in [[energy-availability-floor]] is triggered by the observable signals, not by a diagnostic
label, and is deliberately built that way so that the construct debate cannot be used as a reason not
to refer.

## What is missing

No ultrarunning-specific prevalence or risk data was found. That matters more here than the gap's
size suggests: ultrarunners carry some of the highest training loads in amateur sport, over long
durations at intensities where in-session fuelling is difficult, in a culture that is unusually
tolerant of leanness. Every risk factor points the same way and nobody has measured it. Recorded in
[[where-the-evidence-lives]].
