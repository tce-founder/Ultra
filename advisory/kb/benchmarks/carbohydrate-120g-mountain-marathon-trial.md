# The 120 g/h mountain marathon trial

## The result

Twenty male elite runners, previously gut-trained, were randomised to ingest **120, 90 or 60 g/h**
of carbohydrate during a mountain marathon. The 120 g/h group showed **significantly lower
exercise-induced muscle damage markers 24 hours after the race** than the 90 and 60 g/h groups.

## What it establishes

That in this population, at this event duration and intensity, a very high supervised carbohydrate
intake was **tolerable** and was associated with **less post-race muscle damage** than lower rates.
Both halves are interesting and the first is arguably the more useful: the tolerance finding is what
licenses the gut-training block in [[gut-training-protocol]].

## What it does not establish

- **It is not a performance result.** The outcome measured was a 24-hour post-race damage marker.
  Nothing here says the 120 g/h group finished faster, and the number is nonetheless quoted as a
  performance recommendation more often than not.
- **It does not generalise past its preconditions.** Elite, male, gut-trained, mountain-marathon
  duration. An untrained gut at 120 g/h in a fourteen-hour race is a different experiment and the
  likeliest outcome is the one recorded in [[gi-distress-and-dnf-figures]].
- **It is a small sample.** Twenty, split three ways.
- **It does not tell you the ceiling.** No arm went higher, so "120 is the maximum" is not what was
  tested.

## The field data, which point the other way

Observational studies of what ultrarunners actually consume — real-time food and fluid observation
during a 120 km ultramarathon, and dietary observation across a continuous 24-hour event — report
intakes **well below** these prescribed targets. That gap is the most practically important fact on
this page, and it admits two readings:

1. Runners under-fuel, and the trial shows what is possible with preparation and supervision.
2. Longer, lower-intensity events have a genuinely lower tolerable ceiling than a mountain marathon,
   and the trial's duration is doing more work than its headline suggests.

**Nothing consulted resolves which.** Both are held in [[carbohydrate-fueling-ladder]], and the
practical consequence there is to build the rate from the athlete's own tested tolerance rather than
from either literature.
