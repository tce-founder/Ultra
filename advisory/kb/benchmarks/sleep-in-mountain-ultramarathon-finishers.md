# Sleep in mountain ultramarathon finishers

## The findings

| Finding | Detail |
| ------- | ------ |
| **Race durations** | Mountain 100-milers (Western States, UTMB) take roughly 20–24 h for the fastest finishers and up to about 48 h for most of the field |
| **In-race sleep** | Not all runners sleep during the race; those who do rely on **brief naps** |
| **Post-race wakefulness** | Runners reported recovering a normal state of wakefulness **within about two days** |
| **Sleep extension** | Runners who extended sleep before the race **finished faster** than those who did not |
| **Pacing signal** | An **earlier shift in race pacing** under sleep deprivation predicted later performance in a single-effort ultramarathon |
| **Recovery interaction** | One night of partial sleep deprivation impaired recovery from a high-intensity session in cyclists |

## The survivorship problem, stated plainly

This is a survey of people who **finished**. Every runner whose night ended their race is missing,
and that is the exact group whose behaviour a night strategy is meant to influence. So:

- "Finishers rely on brief naps" is compatible with "runners who needed a long sleep dropped out".
- The sleep-extension association is **an association among finishers**, and the runners who both
  slept badly beforehand and did not finish are not in the denominator.

The finding is still worth acting on, because sleep extension is free and has no downside — the bar
for acting on weak evidence is set by the cost of acting, and here that cost is zero. That reasoning
is recorded explicitly in [[sleep-and-the-night]] so it is not mistaken for confidence in the study.

## The finding with the most practical weight

**Pacing shifts before legs fail.** Under sleep deprivation the observable change is in pacing and,
by extension, judgement — which is the empirical basis for the rule in [[sleep-and-the-night]] and
in the DNF material that irreversible decisions do not get made in the dark. Of everything on this
page it is the item that most changes what an athlete should actually do.
